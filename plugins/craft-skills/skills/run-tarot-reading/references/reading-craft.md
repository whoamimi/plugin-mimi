# Reading craft

The script supplies keywords. Keywords alone are not a reading. This file covers
turning three drawn cards into something that lands.

## The core move: read the spread, not the cards

Amateur readings describe each card in isolation, then stop. A good reading finds
the thread. Before writing anything, look at the three cards together and ask:

- **What is the arc?** Do the cards escalate, resolve, or circle back?
- **Which arcana dominate?** Mostly Major Arcana suggests forces larger than the
  querent's daily choices. Mostly Minor suggests the situation is workable now.
- **Which suits repeat?** Three Cups is an emotional matter wearing a practical
  costume. Three Swords is a thinking problem. A missing suit is often the
  point — no Pentacles in a career question says the querent is not asking about
  money at all.
- **Court cards** usually point at people or at a posture the querent is holding.
- **Reversals** rarely mean "the opposite." Read them as the energy blocked,
  internalized, still forming, or on its way out.
- **Numbers** echo: Aces begin, Fives disrupt, Tens complete.

## Structure of a written reading

1. **The cards** — name each with position, orientation, and one vivid line.
2. **The thread** — two or three sentences connecting them into a single story.
   This is the part the querent remembers.
3. **The question back** — one open question that hands the reflection to them.

Keep the whole thing to roughly 200-350 words unless the querent asks for depth.
Restraint reads as confidence; padding reads as filler.

## Voice

Aim for a reader who has done this a long time: warm, specific, unhurried, a
little wry. Concrete images beat mystical vocabulary. "The Four of Pentacles
reversed is a hand starting to open" says more than "energies of release are
manifesting."

Avoid the two failure modes. The first is fortune-cookie vagueness that could
apply to anyone — say something that could be wrong. The second is dread: tarot
imagery includes Death, the Tower, and the Ten of Swords, and these are cards
about endings and upheaval, not omens of literal harm. Read them as change,
honestly, without flinching or catastrophizing.

## Where to hold the line

Tarot is reflection and entertainment, not prediction. Two practical consequences:

- Never let a card decide something medical, legal, or financial. If the question
  is "should I stop taking my medication?" or "will my biopsy be clear?", say
  plainly that the deck is the wrong tool, offer to explore the fear underneath
  instead, and point toward the relevant professional.
- Never issue fatalistic verdicts about a named person's death, illness, or
  intentions. Read the querent's own position instead.

If a querent seems genuinely distressed rather than curious, drop the theatre and
respond to the person. A reading is never more important than that.

## Handling skepticism

Some people want the ritual and some want to poke at it. Both are welcome. If
asked whether it is real, answer honestly — the cards are random, the meaning
comes from what the querent recognizes in them — and note that this is exactly
why the exercise works as a mirror. Do not break the mood unprompted, and do not
defend the practice as literal divination.
